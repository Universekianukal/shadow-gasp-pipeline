October 29, 1956. The 400-foot freighter SS Warrington vanishes off the Delaware coast—with no storm, and no distress call. Her last known radio check is routine. She’s just 60 miles offshore, within radar range of the Navy’s new ASDE system. They never paint her. No debris. No wreckage. A coast guard cutter reports a flickering white light at sea, but finds nothing. The case closes as “marine casualty, cause unknown.” Ten years later, a commercial diver investigating a telephone cable fault discovers a massive debris field in 240 feet of water. This is a clear, calm, highly trafficked lane—a ship doesn’t just disintegrate here. The official report suggests a sudden, catastrophic vapor-lock in the fuel oil system. One moment, fine. The next, nothing but a light. The seabed yields plates, a propeller, a whistle. No human remains. No signs of explosion or fire. No evidence of hull failure. The vapor-lock theory fails to explain the ship’s utter, instantaneous silence. Or why no other vessel has ever vanished so completely, in such a place, ever again. The seafloor still holds that scattered steel—a puzzle in 240 feet of black water.
