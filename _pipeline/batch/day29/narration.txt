October 1900. Forty-five meters down, off the island of Antikythera, sponge diver Elias Stadiatis claws back to the surface screaming about a pile of corpses on the seafloor. His captain sends him back down. They're not bodies — they're bronze, corroded by two thousand years underwater. A Roman-era shipwreck, sunk around 60 BC. Over the next year, divers haul up marble statues, coins, glass — and one blackened lump the size of a shoebox. Two of the divers never walk again. One dies. The bends. Nobody understood it yet. The lump sits in a museum for months, ignored. Then in 1902, archaeologist Valerios Stais notices something impossible: a gear wheel, embedded in the rock, teeth cut to a millimeter. Seventy years of X-rays later, scientists count 30 bronze gears inside — a machine that predicted eclipses, tracked the Olympic calendar, modeled the moon's wobble. Nothing this precise exists again for over a thousand years. Nobody knows who built it. No blueprints. No second machine. No workshop. Just 82 corroded fragments — and the growing certainty that whoever made this had knowledge the rest of history simply lost.
