On November 19, 1934, a 20-year-old artist named Everett Ruess carved the word NEMO into a sandstone wall in southeastern Utah. Then he walked into the canyonlands and was never seen again. Ruess had been wandering the desert for four years, painting watercolors, sleeping under the stars, leading two burros named Curly and Pretty. He wrote letters home to Los Angeles describing canyon walls that 'burned with color.' He was 5'6", 120 pounds, carrying a pack full of art supplies and journals. Three weeks after his last known camp in Davis Gulch, a search party found his burros — still alive, still saddled. A cache of his belongings sat nearby. His sketchbook. His journal. His food. But no body. No blood. No sign of struggle. He had signed the name NEMO — Latin for 'no one' — across the sandstone for months, as if scripting his own erasure. In 2009, a Navajo man led researchers to a grave near Comb Ridge. Bones. A shirt. Buttons matching Ruess's gear. The family submitted DNA. The result: not him. Someone else entirely, buried in the same era, in the same clothes. The NEMO carvings kept appearing for decades after 1934 — some scratched into rock faces 50 miles from where he vanished. Some proven authentic. Some proven fake. Nobody knows which ones Everett made. Or who made the others. Or where his bones are. The desert kept 'no one.'
