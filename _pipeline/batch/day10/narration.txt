November 24th, 1931. Fifty miles off Point Barrow, Alaska, the crew of the SS Baychimo abandons ship — trapped in Arctic pack ice, deemed a total loss. Ten days later, an Inuit hunter runs into their camp: the ship is still floating, forty-five miles away, drifting alone. The crew treks back, boards her, hauls off furs worth nearly one million dollars today. Then another blizzard rolls in. When it clears, the Baychimo is gone again. This time salvage crews go looking. They fail. Storm after storm. Over the next 38 years, sailors keep spotting her — 1933, 1934, 1939 — a rusting steel ghost drifting the Bering Sea, cargo untouched, tables still set below deck. In 1939, a group boards her and finds everything frozen in place, like the crew just stepped out. No one ever tows her home. The last confirmed sighting: July 1969, thirty-six miles off Alaska, still afloat after thirty-eight years in ice that sinks ships in days. Then she vanishes for good. No wreck found. No debris. No official end. Just empty water where a ghost ship used to be.
