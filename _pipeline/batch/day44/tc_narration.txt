November 19th, 1961. Michael Rockefeller ties two empty gas cans to his waist and dives into the Arafura Sea. Behind him, a capsized catamaran drifts three miles off the coast of Netherlands New Guinea. His companion, anthropologist René Wassing, stays with the wreck. Michael swims for shore alone. He is 23 years old, heir to one of America's greatest fortunes. He is never seen again.

Wassing is rescued eleven hours later. Michael is not. His father, New York Governor Nelson Rockefeller, flies in personally. Dutch and Australian forces launch the largest search in New Guinea's history — planes, patrol boats, a thousand villagers combing sixty miles of coastline. Nothing. No body. No wreckage. Officials rule it drowning, or crocodiles.

But in 2014, journalist Carl Hoffman spends years inside Asmat villages and hears a different story: Michael made it to shore alive. And the Asmat, still avenging a Dutch massacre from 1958, killed him — and ritually consumed him, as they had consumed enemies for generations.

Villagers name a man. They describe his glasses. No remains were ever recovered. No charges were ever filed. Sixty years later, nobody knows what really happened on that beach.
