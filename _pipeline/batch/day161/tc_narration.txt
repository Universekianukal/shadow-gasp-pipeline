7:01 PM, June 23, 1955, 13,000 feet over Lake Michigan. Northwest Orient Flight 2501, a DC-4 with 58 souls, blinks off radar. No Mayday. Wreckage is found not in the water, but on the land. A single seat cushion, a shredded passenger headrest, recovered from the Michigan shoreline. For years, they’ve all been looking at the deep. They find the clues on the beach. A life jacket in a farmer's field fifteen miles inland. The official navy report? “Cause unknown.” The lake keeps its dead. A woman from Grand Rapids files the only lawsuit against the airline, claiming she saw the plane’s running lights fall below the horizon and then a flash of fire, a story the government dismisses. Salvagers chase a ghost on sonar for decades, convinced the fuselage lies in 300-foot depths. They find nothing. The cause – structural failure? Sabotage? Electrical storm? – officially sealed for 75 years and counting. The largest commercial air disaster of its time is now the largest unsolved aviation mystery in American waters. The lake never gave them back.
