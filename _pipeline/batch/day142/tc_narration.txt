On March 17, 1970, the 213 residents of Mont-Dragon, a remote French Alpine village, simply stopped existing. No phone calls. No mail. The single road in and out, untouched by tires or footprints after a fresh snowfall. Prefectural authorities, alerted by a missed tax payment, found a ghost town. Tables set with meals, livestock in pens, banknotes on shop counters. Every personal belonging, every family photograph, remained. Yet not one person, from the mayor to the newborn. The official investigation concluded ‘voluntary, collective relocation’ without stating a destination. No missing persons reports were ever filed by next of kin. No graves in the local cemetery post-1969. And in the village church registry, the final entry, dated March 16, is a single line: ‘The flock has departed.’ It cites no presiding priest.
