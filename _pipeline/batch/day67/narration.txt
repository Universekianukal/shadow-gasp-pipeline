February 2, 2009. A woman walks her dog across the West Mesa — 92 acres of empty New Mexico desert slated for a housing development. The dog stops. Starts digging. A human thigh bone surfaces from the sand. Within weeks, the count climbs. Eleven women. All buried in shallow graves across a single 200-by-300-foot stretch of arid scrubland. They're not random. Every victim vanished between 2003 and 2005. Most were sex workers working Albuquerque's Central Avenue corridor. Many struggled with addiction. Several were mothers. The youngest was 15. Here's the reversal. The burial ground sat 100 yards from a construction site active during the disappearances. Workers came and went daily. Nobody saw anything. Whoever did this buried eleven bodies in broad daylight, in soil so hard it had to be broken with picks — and vanished without a trace. Investigators named persons of interest. Lorenzo Montoya — killed in 2006 by a pimp. Fred Reynolds — a police reserve officer who died in 2010 before questioning. Joseph Blea — linked by circumstantial evidence, never charged. A $100,000 reward still sits unclaimed. Albuquerque's largest crime scene is now a fenced lot behind a subdivision. Eleven women, one burial ground, and a killer who may still be breathing.
