On April 19, 1956, a 47-year-old Royal Navy frogman slips into Portsmouth Harbour and vanishes beneath the hull of a Soviet warship. The cruiser Ordzhonikidze is docked for a goodwill visit. Aboard her: Nikita Khrushchev himself. The diver is Commander Lionel Crabb — two George Medals, a legend of underwater demolition in the Second World War. MI6 has sent him to inspect the Soviet propeller assembly, a classified intelligence target. He carries a single re-breather. Two hundred minutes of air. His handler, Bernard Smith, waits on a nearby boat. Crabb descends at 8:40 PM. He is never seen alive again. The Soviets later report their sailors spotted a diver near the hull at 1:15 AM — four and a half hours after he entered the water. Then nothing. Fourteen months pass. In June 1957, a headless body in a Royal Navy diving suit washes ashore near Pilsey Island, 73 miles from Portsmouth. The head is never recovered. Identification rests entirely on a surgical scar on the left knee. No dental records. No fingerprints. No face. MI6's director is forced to resign. But the coroner lists the cause of death as unknown. No bullet wounds. No stab marks. The suit is intact. Crabb's family never accepts the body was his. And the head — wherever it went — has never been found.
