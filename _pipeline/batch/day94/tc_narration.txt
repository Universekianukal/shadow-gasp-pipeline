On June 22, 1953, a thirteen-year-old Brooklyn newsboy dropped a customer's nickel on the sidewalk. It cracked open. Inside: a tiny photograph, no bigger than a fingernail, covered in columns of numbers. The boy showed his sister. His sister called a detective. The detective called the FBI. For four years, the nickel sat in an evidence drawer. The numbers were a Soviet one-time pad cipher — unbreakable without the key. The Bureau had a dead coin, a dead code, and no name. Then in 1957, a Soviet operative named Reino Häyhänen walked into the American embassy in Paris and defected. He identified the nickel's handler: a man in Brooklyn calling himself Emil Goldfus. His real name was Colonel Rudolf Abel — one of the KGB's top agents in North America. Abel was arrested at the Hotel Latham in Manhattan on June 21, 1957 — almost exactly four years after the nickel fell. Convicted. Sentenced to 45 years. Served five. In 1962, Abel was traded on the Glienicke Bridge for Francis Gary Powers, the U-2 pilot shot down over Soviet territory. But Reino Häyhänen — the defector who cracked the case — never lived to see it. He died on a Pennsylvania highway in 1961. Car off the road. Cause never determined.
