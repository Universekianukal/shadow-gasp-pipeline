Christmas Eve, 1945. Fayetteville, West Virginia. George Sodder wakes to smoke at 1 a.m. Five of his nine children are still upstairs. He runs for the ladder always kept against the house — it's gone. Found later, 75 feet away, in a ravine. The phone line: not damaged. Cut. The fire department, based just two and a half miles away, doesn't arrive for eight hours. By the time they do, the house is ash. No bones. No teeth. No belt buckles. Nothing. Investigators rule it faulty wiring and close the case. But George had made enemies — loud complaints about Mussolini to a local Italian businessman months earlier. Weeks before the fire, an insurance salesman warned him his house would 'go up in smoke' over his politics. The Sodders search for forty-three years. They build a roadside billboard: photographs, a reward, questions nobody answers. In 1968, a letter arrives with no return address. Inside: a photograph of a grown man who looks exactly like their son Louis. The billboard stays up until 1989. Nobody has ever explained where five children went.
