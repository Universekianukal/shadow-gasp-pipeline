Three days after closing on their dream house, a letter arrives. No return address. Handwritten. It reads: "Do you know the history of the house? Do you know what lies within the walls?" Derek and Maria Broaddus paid $1.355 million for 657 Boulevard in Westfield, New Jersey, June 2014. Six bedrooms, colonial revival, built 1905. They plan to raise three kids there. The second letter escalates: "My grandfather watched this house in the 1920s and my father watched in the 1960s. It is now my time." The writer signs every letter "The Watcher." Claims to know the children's names. Calls them "young blood." The Broadduses never spend one night inside. They hire private investigators. Police run DNA off envelope glue — inconclusive. Handwriting analysts find nothing usable. A neighboring family gets sued, dragged through headlines, never charged. Here's the reversal: the case never gets solved. Instead the Broadduses sue the previous sellers for concealing the letters, and settle — a legal fix for a mystery nobody can close. In 2019, they sell 657 Boulevard at a $400,000 loss. The letters stop. The Watcher is never identified. Somewhere in Westfield, someone still knows exactly which windows face which bedrooms.
