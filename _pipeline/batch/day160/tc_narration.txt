The SS Fidelity drifts into Halifax harbor on a clear April morning in 1988. No distress signal was ever sent. Coast Guard boarding parties find a full breakfast congealed on the galley table, coffee still warm in the pot. The entire 14-man crew is gone. Logbook entries are normal, right up until 0600 hours. No lifeboats launched. No signs of struggle or storm damage. A single cigarette, still smoldering, rests in an ashtray on the bridge. The engines are idling. The ship's cargo—grain bound for Liverpool—is untouched and securely battened down. Two million dollars' worth of modern ship, a skeleton crew running a skeleton. The investigation focuses on the crew. No criminal records. No financial troubles. No links between the men beyond their shared employment. A search of their quarters finds personal effects, family photos, wallets with cash. They simply vanished between one log entry and the next, in calm seas with clear skies. The official inquiry cites 'unknown marine casualty' and closes the file. But fishermen in the area that morning report seeing nothing. No flares. No boats. A ship that size doesn't just swallow fourteen men without a trace. The Atlantic keeps the Fidelity's secret. The only clue that remains is spectral: the echo of the idling engine, humming to an empty sea.
