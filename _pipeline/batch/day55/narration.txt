August 1994. A police report calls it a simple theft — smoke detectors missing from an apartment complex storage closet. Officers find a teenager, calm, holding tools, claiming he's doing government research. They let him go. Ten months later, that same teenager — David Hahn, age 17, Eagle Scout — makes an anonymous call to the EPA hazmat hotline. He says a friend has radioactive material in his shed. He's talking about himself. Agents arrive at his mother's Commerce Township, Michigan property. Their Geiger counter pins past the top of its scale — a thousand times normal background radiation. Inside the plywood shed: a homemade breeder reactor. For three years David had cracked open thousands of lantern mantles for thorium, stripped smoke detectors for americium, and scraped radium paint off clock dials — using forged NRC letterhead to request samples by mail. Federal responders feared a meltdown. The entire shed was cut apart, sealed in 39 barrels, and buried as low-level nuclear waste in a Utah desert facility. Up to 40,000 neighbors may have been exposed to trace contamination. No study ever tracked them. David got his Eagle Scout badge anyway — for atomic energy. He died in 2016, age 39, alone in his apartment. His last known photo shows marks on his face officials never explained.
