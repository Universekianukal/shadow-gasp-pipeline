Seventy-eight meters of tunnel. Six months of digging. One hundred and sixty million reais — gone. August 8, 2005. Fortaleza, Brazil. Banco Central employees arrive Monday morning and find the vault door open. Not forced. Opened. Two rows of 50-real notes, carefully restacked to hide the gap. Behind a wall, a hole. Through that hole: a tunnel — 78 meters long, half a meter wide, reinforced with wood, wired with electric lighting and air conditioning. The entrance sat two streets away — a rented house leased by a man who registered a fake landscaping company. Neighbors thought he ran a quiet business. He even delivered flowers to them. Between six and twenty men drilled through six feet of steel-reinforced concrete over a single weekend, working in short bursts to dodge vibration sensors. The take: 160 million reais. Roughly 70 million U.S. dollars. Uninsured — central banks don't carry insurance. Then the case breaks open — but not the way anyone expects. Police arrest eight suspects within months. One turns up dead in a car trunk. Another vanishes after posting bail. Of 160 million, only 20 million is ever recovered. The rest flows through used-car lots, phantom real estate, small businesses across three countries. 140 million reais. Still out there. Untraceable. Unaccounted. Spent.
