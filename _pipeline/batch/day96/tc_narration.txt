On August 7, 1994, rain fell from a cloudless sky over Oakville, Washington. Not water. Gelatinous blobs, each half the size of a grain of rice, blanketing ten square miles of farmland. Over the next three weeks, it fell six more times. 66-year-old Sunny Beveridge touched it. Within 24 hours, vertigo and blurred vision. A police officer who had blobs land on his bare hands developed a respiratory infection so severe he couldn't work. Samples went to a lab in Olympia. The results were impossible. The blobs contained human white blood cells — two types of human leukocytes, cells that exist only inside a living immune system. No DNA. No nucleus. No biological mechanism that could produce them. One microbiologist proposed decomposed jellyfish, possibly ejected from a military bombing range twenty miles off the Pacific coast and scattered by wind. Except the nearest range is over open ocean, and jellyfish cells don't survive atmospheric dispersal. The blobs stopped falling in September 1994. No agency investigated further. The samples were eventually discarded. No archive remains — just lab reports in an Olympia basement and residents who never got an answer.
