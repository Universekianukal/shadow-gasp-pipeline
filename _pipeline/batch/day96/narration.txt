The TV had been running for nearly three years when they found her. January 25, 2006. Housing officials force open a bolted flat in Wood Green, North London. Inside: a bedsit frozen since late 2003. The television still on, still tuned to BBC1, still drawing electricity — 28 months of a dead woman's power bill, auto-debited, never flagged. On the sofa: skeletal remains. On the floor: unopened Christmas presents. She was Joyce Carol Vincent. Thirty-eight. Once vivacious, well-connected, with friends across London. By 2003, she had withdrawn. Cut contact with half-siblings. Moved without telling anyone. Friends later said they had been searching — one had contacted police. No missing person report was ever filed. The coroner could not determine cause of death. Too much time. No forced entry. No foul play. No disease found. Nothing. She died surrounded by a working television, wrapped gifts, a lock that held — while every system designed to notice a dead tenant failed in perfect synchronization. The housing association chased arrears by letter. The utility company kept billing. Neighbors assumed the TV noise was normal. BBC1 kept broadcasting into an empty room. And the question that remains — how she actually died — was buried with her.
