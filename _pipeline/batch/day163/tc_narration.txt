March 3rd, 1876. Over a 100-yard stretch of Kentucky farm, hunks of red, fleshy slime began to fall from a clear blue sky. It landed with soft, wet plops. Mrs. Crouch said it smelled like mutton. The local doctor bagged samples. His analysis: striated muscle fiber and lung tissue. It was fresh meat. Theories came fast. Mass bird vomit? Vulture disgorgement? A gruesome slaughterhouse prank? The twist: not a single report of dead animals, no flocks of birds, no logical source within miles. The Smithsonian took the case. Their final verdict? It was nostoc—a type of blue-green algae that forms gelatinous blobs. The solution felt stranger than the mystery. But the Smithsonian’s own chemical analysis contradicted them. Their tests showed no cellulose, no plant matter. It was animal tissue. They published the nostoc answer anyway. The meat was never identified. The samples are gone. Over a century later, the question remains: in a cloudless sky, where did 100 pounds of fresh flesh come from?
