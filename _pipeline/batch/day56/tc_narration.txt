June, 1939. A ten-year-old orphan girl claps a hand over her mouth mid-sentence — terrified to speak. She's fluent. Perfectly normal. She's about to be told otherwise.

At the Soldiers and Sailors Orphans' Home in Davenport, Iowa, University of Iowa researcher Wendell Johnson sends grad student Mary Tudor to test 22 children, ages 5 to 15. Half are pulled aside and told their speech is failing — criticized for every hesitation, ordered to stop stuttering immediately. The other half, actual stutterers, are told they sound completely fine.

The goal: prove stuttering is learned, not born. It works. Fluent children start stammering. Covering their mouths. Going silent in class.

Here's the twist. The paper is finished — and never published. Mary Tudor is so ashamed of what she did, she seals the results herself. For sixty-two years, nobody outside Iowa knows it happened.

It surfaces in 2001, when a journalist digs the files out of a university archive. Iowa issues a public apology. Six survivors sue the state.

2007: a $925,000 settlement.

But no one has ever tracked down all 22 children. No one knows how many spent their whole lives stuttering — never told why.
