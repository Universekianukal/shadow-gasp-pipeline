April 3, 1989. An alarm tripped at 3:17 AM inside the Carnavalet Museum in Paris. Guards found a single display case smashed. The object taken wasn't a jewel or a painting. It was a 10-foot-long blood-red flannel scarf. The silk accessory belonged to Isadora Duncan. The dancer was famously killed in 1927 when an identical scarf she was wearing snagged on the wheel of an open-top car. The original was her death shroud. So why steal a morbid replica, valued at under $200? The museum's security was advanced. Only one other artifact — an unrelated 18th-century fan — was disturbed. The thief ignored priceless works nearby. He left no fingerprints. No demanding ransom note followed. The case was quietly closed within months. The scarf—and the thief's motive—simply vanished. Today, the empty case remains at the Carnavalet. Is it the world's most morbid souvenir? A deranged tribute? Or does the phantom of 1989 still possess the only object that could explain his obsession?
