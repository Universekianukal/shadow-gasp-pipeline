The last radio message from the Sea Dragon came March 24, 1939. Position: 1,800 miles west of Midway Island. "Southerly gale. Heavy rain. All well." Richard Halliburton was the most famous adventurer in America. He'd swum the Panama Canal, climbed the Matterhorn, slept atop the Great Pyramid. Survived every stunt. Now he wanted to sail a 75-foot Chinese junk across the Pacific. The Sea Dragon was built in Hong Kong by traditional shipwrights who had never designed for open ocean. Halliburton added a diesel engine and a shortwave radio. Crew of seven, including his partner Paul Mooney. Budget: $10,000 — about $220,000 today. March 24. The last transmission. Then silence. No Navy search. No Coast Guard search. His publisher hired one private vessel. It found nothing. No wreckage, no debris, no bodies, no oil slick. Eight men vanished from the ocean as if they'd never been on it. That same day, a weather station recorded 35-foot swells in that corridor. A 75-foot junk in 35-foot seas — and the message said "all well." Either he didn't know what was coming, or the message wasn't his. Eleven weeks later, a fishing vessel off the Philippines reported a Chinese junk drifting with no crew. No investigation followed. The report was filed and forgotten. The Sea Dragon has never been found. No wreck. No trace. Somewhere on the Pacific floor — or somewhere much closer than anyone looked.
