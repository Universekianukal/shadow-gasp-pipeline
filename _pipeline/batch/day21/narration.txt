Lisbon, December 1925. A bank clerk lays two five-hundred-escudo notes side by side. Same serial number. Same paper. Same ink. Neither one is fake. Nine months earlier, a 27-year-old con man named Alves dos Reis walked into Waterlow & Sons, London's most trusted banknote printer, carrying forged contracts stamped with the Bank of Portugal's seal. He claimed a secret loan to Portuguese Angola. Secrecy, he said, even from the bank itself. Waterlow didn't just believe him. They printed 200,000 real banknotes worth 100 million escudos — using the exact same plates as genuine currency. Reis funneled the cash through a shell bank he built from nothing, buying property, shares, even a controlling stake in the real Bank of Portugal. For months, the fraud was undetectable. These weren't forgeries. They were real money, made real by the one printer trusted to make it. The crisis crashed the escudo, gutted public trust, and helped trigger the 1926 coup that ended Portugal's democracy for 48 years. Reis served five years. Millions in duplicate notes were never traced. No one has ever confirmed exactly how much of Portugal's 1925 money supply was his.
