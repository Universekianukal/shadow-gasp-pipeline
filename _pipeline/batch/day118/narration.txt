The radio crackles with a final, brief transmission from the bridge of Le Griffon on September 7th, 1999. The crew of the 35-meter French meteorological research vessel are calm. They are 300 miles off the coast of Senegal, tracking Hurricane Gert. Hours later, they are scheduled to send another data burst. Silence. A French Navy Orion flies a search grid. They spot something 130 miles from the last known position: an orange life raft. It is empty. No debris. No oil slick. The vessel’s Emergency Position-Indicating Radio Beacon is never activated. The official inquiry classifies it as loss due to extreme weather. But veteran sailors point to the data: Gert was weakening, with winds below hurricane force in the vessel’s last known sector. The ship was modern, ice-strengthened, built for the Southern Ocean. In 2009, a satellite image analyst claims a blip on declassified imagery from two days after the disappearance—85 miles from the search zone. A shape the exact length of Le Griffon. The French government dismisses it as a processing artifact. Forty-two crew simply gone, with a raft left behind. No mayday. No final signal. Just an empty orange dot in a vast, calm sea.
