S-T-E-N-D-E-C. The Santiago operator reads it once, asks for clarification. Gets it again. Asks again. Gets it a third time. Then silence. August 2, 1947 — British South American Airways Flight CS-59, the Lancastrian airliner Star Dust, eleven souls aboard, crossing the Andes from Buenos Aires to Santiago. 17:41 local time. The last transmission ever received. STENDEC, sent in Morse code, means nothing. No recognized aviation abbreviation, no distress signal, no word in any language. Search planes scan 200,000 square kilometers of Andean peaks. Nothing. Not a wing, not a body, not a scrap of fabric. Eight days later, the search is abandoned. Theories accumulate — an acronym for 'Severe Turbulence Encountered, Navigation Equipment Compromised,' a Morse misinterpretation, a dying operator's garbled farewell. None confirmed. Fifty-one years pass. In 1998, climbers on Mount Tupungato find a Rolls-Royce Merlin engine half-buried in a glacier at 15,000 feet. Star Dust. The jet stream — unknown to aviators in 1947 — had pushed the crew off course inside cloud cover. They believed they'd cleared the mountains. They were descending straight into one. But STENDEC? Still unsolved. No definitive decode has ever been accepted. The last word of eleven people remains a cipher.
