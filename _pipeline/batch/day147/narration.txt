February 21, 2004. Rebel mortar shells land on a Ugandan army base. The commanding officer, a man named Peter Kerim, responds with a chilling order. He wants 100 civilians killed for each dead soldier. A column of armored vehicles rolls toward the displaced persons camp at Barlonyo, where 5,000 had sought shelter from the 20-year war. They claim they are securing the area. Then the soldiers open fire. Families are shot in the open fields. Shacks are set ablaze with people inside. A Red Cross worker, trying to flee with a woman and child, is executed by a direct pistol shot to his head. The official toll is 301 dead. Survivors whisper the number is closer to 400. The army blames the rebels. A government inquiry is formed, then quietly folds. It recommends compensation, which never comes. The main suspect, Peter Kerim, is not charged. He retires on a full pension. To this day, no one has been held accountable for the Barlonyo massacre. The case file sits in a drawer. The mass graves remain, marked by simple wooden crosses in the tall grass. And the survivors still ask one question: when will the Ugandan army be held to account for its own war crimes?
