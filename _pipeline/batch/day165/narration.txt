One night in August 2016, the Olympic diving pool in Rio turns a putrid, murky green. Swimmers emerge with burning eyes and foggy vision. Officials blame a mysterious algae bloom. But then the water polo pool–connected by the same massive filtration system–stays clear and blue. The answer isn't biology. It's chemistry. Two workers accidentally dumped 80 liters of hydrogen peroxide into the diving well. It reacted with the chlorine, destroying its disinfectant power and allowing metals in the concrete basin to oxidize. The water turned toxic. The emergency fix is surreal: 10,000 liters of chlorine, manually injected. Athletes compete in a chemical soup. Days later, a green tide returns, this time to the water polo pool. The reversal breaks the pattern: after correcting the first error, an Olympic-level mistake happens again. The final, eerie detail: official reports blamed only "human error," but never explained how the same system failed twice, or why the identical hydrogen peroxide tanks were left unlabeled next to the chlorine. The diving boards stand empty over water permanently stained in Olympic memory.
