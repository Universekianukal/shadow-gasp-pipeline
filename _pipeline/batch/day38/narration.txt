The SS Edmund Fitzgerald was 729 feet long when it vanished from radar on November 10, 1975 — 29 men to the bottom of Lake Superior, no distress call. 26,000 tons of iron ore pellets from Superior, Wisconsin toward Detroit. By 3:30 PM, winds hit 67 mph. Waves reached 25 feet. The Arthur M. Anderson, ten miles behind, tracked the blip through the storm. At 7:10 PM, Captain McSorley radioed the Anderson: we are holding our own. Ten minutes later — the blip vanished. No mayday. No flare. Twenty-nine men, gone in what looked like seconds. The wreck was found 17 days later, 530 feet down. The ship had split in two — bow and stern 170 feet apart. The Coast Guard blamed failed hatch covers. But the Fitzgerald had reported flooding and lost radar hours earlier — and kept sailing. It passed within three miles of Caribou Island, where the lakebed rises to 39 feet. The loaded ship drew 27 feet. If she scraped the shoal, the damage would've been silent — until it wasn't. The bell was recovered in 1995. The bodies never surfaced. Lake Superior is too cold for the bacteria that make the dead float. The exact mechanism remains disputed. The ship may have gone from holding its own to gone in under thirty seconds.
