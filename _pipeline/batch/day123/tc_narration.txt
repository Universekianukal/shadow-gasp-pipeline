September 16, 1890. Louis Le Prince boards the 13:42 train in Dijon, bound for Paris. He carries the patents for the world's first moving-picture camera. He has a meeting that will prove it. The train doors close. His brother-in-law waves from the platform. Le Prince is never seen again. Not on the 5:05 in Paris. Not on any passenger list. Authorities search the train, the tracks, the entire route. Nothing. His luggage, his camera prototypes, his business plans — all gone with him. He was supposed to demonstrate his invention to investors in America, beating Thomas Edison to the patent. The case closes quickly: presumed suicide. No body. No note. No evidence. Two years later, Edison's agent patents a nearly identical device. Le Prince's own son testifies he believes his father was murdered for the invention. In 2003, archivists found a faded 1890 photograph in the Paris police archives. It shows a drowned man fished from the Seine. The face is blurred, the date is September 1890. The police label is clear: "Unidentified Drowned Man." The records show it was never shown to Le Prince's family. The file was marked "case closed" and filed away. The first man to capture motion was erased by it.
