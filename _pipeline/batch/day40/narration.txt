July 5th, 1919. A farmer finds 21-year-old Bella Wright lying dead on a lonely Leicestershire lane, blood crusted around her left eye. Crows have pecked at the wound. Police call it a bicycle accident — a bird strike, a fall. Case closed in a day. Then the post-mortem. A single bullet, fired from close range, passed clean through her skull. Witnesses remember a man on a distinctive green bicycle riding beside her that evening. He vanishes. No name. No trail. Seven months pass. February 1920 — dredgers in Leicester's River Soar pull up a green bicycle, deliberately weighted down. The frame number leads police to Ronald Light, a 34-year-old engineer. Light admits it: he rode with Bella that night. Then he panicked, he says — sank the bike, ditched the gun, said nothing for seven months. He denies pulling the trigger. At trial, defended by Marshall Hall, the jury deliberates barely twenty minutes. Not guilty. The revolver that killed Bella Wright was never found. Neither was a motive. Light lived another 55 years. He never explained why he really ran.
