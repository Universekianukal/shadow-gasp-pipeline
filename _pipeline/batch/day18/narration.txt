June 1947. Strait of Malacca. Two American ships, Silver Star and City of Baltimore, pick up a broken Morse code distress call. All officers including captain dead. Lying in chartroom and bridge. Possibly whole crew dead. Then, seconds later: I die. Then silence.

Silver Star finds her adrift, no visible damage. Boarding crew climb aboard the SS Ourang Medan. Every man is dead. Frozen mid-motion. Faces locked in terror. Mouths open. Arms raised, like they're still fighting something off. The ship's dog lies dead too, teeth bared. No wounds anywhere. No blood.

They rig a tow line. Smoke starts pouring from the hold. They cut it loose and row for their lives. Minutes later, Ourang Medan explodes and sinks, every body still aboard.

Theories stack up fast: leaked nerve gas smuggled from Japanese wartime stockpiles. Carbon monoxide. Methane pockets in the cargo.

Here's the twist nobody expects: no shipping registry on Earth has ever listed a vessel named Ourang Medan. No wreck has ever been found. No confirmed radio logs. No survivors. Just secondhand newspaper accounts, decades old, with no primary source behind them.

Nobody can prove the ship sank.

Nobody can prove it ever sailed at all.
