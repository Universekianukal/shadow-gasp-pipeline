Seven bursts of automated data. Every hour, for six hours, after the plane vanished. Satellite pings from a Boeing 777 with 239 souls aboard, flying nowhere. They call them 'handshakes'—digital hellos to confirm the aircraft's systems are still online. Engineers at Inmarsat realize the satellite isn't receiving positions or messages—only these bare acknowledgments. Each ping's signal delay reveals an arc of possible flight paths, each a thousand miles long. The plane flew for seven hours after its last known contact. Search teams map a ghost flight over the southern Indian Ocean, one ping at a time. The final arc ends in one of the most remote, deep ocean basins on Earth. But the breakthrough isn't a solution—it's an ocean-sized mystery. The pings stop. The plane's wreckage remains unfound. No black boxes. No debris field that matches the final ping's coordinates. The data points to a crash zone. Yet the sea refuses to give up a single piece of the fuselage from the final predicted location.
