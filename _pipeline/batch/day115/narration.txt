September 18, 1961. 12:10 AM. A UN DC-6 carrying Secretary-General Dag Hammarskjöld circles Ndola airport in Northern Rhodesia. It never lands. Six miles from the runway, it drops into the bush. Fifteen people die instantly. One survives — Sergeant Harold Julian. He tells rescuers he heard an explosion before impact. He dies twelve days later. The official inquiry rules pilot error. No flight recorder is ever found. But the wreckage reveals something the report buries — small, concentrated holes in the fuselage, consistent with shrapnel from an external blast. Witnesses on the ground report a second aircraft — a jet, with running lights. One describes a flash in the sky. Ndola air traffic control logs a call from someone claiming to be Hammarskjöld, delaying the search by nearly fifteen hours. No one investigates the discrepancy. Sixty years later, a UN panel calls external attack plausible. A name surfaces — Jan van Risseghem, a Belgian mercenary pilot who flew for Katangan separatists. His own family claims he confessed. Van Risseghem died in 2007. He took whatever he knew with him. The UN has never opened a criminal investigation. The answer sits in files three governments still refuse to declassify.
