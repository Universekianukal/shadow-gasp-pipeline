Durban Harbour, July 26th, 1909. Claude Sawyer wakes up screaming for the third night straight. In his dream: a sword-wielding figure rising from a blood-red sea, and his ship, the SS Waratah, rolling over and sinking. Shaken, he begs to leave the voyage. The captain won't turn back, but lets him off at Durban. Sawyer walks down the gangway. Two days later, the Waratah sails on toward Cape Town carrying 211 people. She's spotted one last time on July 27th, by the steamer Clan MacIntyre near the Bashee River mouth. No distress signal. No wreckage. No lifeboats. No bodies. Search ships combed 14,000 square miles of ocean for months. Nothing. Investigators later learn something worse: the Waratah had already listed dangerously on her maiden voyage, tilting so far passengers nearly went overboard. The owners knew. She sailed anyway. For over a century, wreck hunters have chased her ghost — sonar hits, rusted hulls, false hope every time. None ever confirmed. Somewhere off South Africa's Wild Coast, the Waratah and 211 people are still down there. Or they aren't. Nobody knows.
