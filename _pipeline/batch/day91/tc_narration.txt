March 18, 2003. A handwritten note arrives at Iraq's Central Bank in Baghdad. Saddam Hussein's signature. One demand: hand over the reserves. His son Qusay stands inside the vault with a personal guard and a pistol on the table. The governor stalls. Qusay doesn't blink. Within hours, workers load $900 million in U.S. bills and $100 million in euros — shrink-wrapped bricks stacked on wooden pallets, driven out in three flatbed trucks into the Baghdad night. One day before American bombs fall. Saddam is emptying his own central bank — technically legal under Iraqi law. His governor's signature is on every withdrawal slip. After Baghdad collapses, U.S. troops raid Saddam's palaces. They find $650 million in a hut behind one palace. Another $132 million inside a farmhouse wall. That's $782 million recovered. Then it gets worse. Soldiers from the 3rd Infantry Division — the very unit that found the cash — start smuggling bricks home. One sergeant ships $600,000 to his wife in the States. Another buries bundles in his backyard in South Carolina. The protectors became the thieves. And roughly $138 million has never been found. No trace. No paper trail. Just gone.
