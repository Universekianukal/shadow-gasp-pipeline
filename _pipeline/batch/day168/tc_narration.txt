A gold bar wrapped in black plastic falls from the sky. August 31st, 1994. A group of Japanese tourists in Malaysia's Taman Negara jungle watch it land near their path. They assume it's a prank, leave it. Two days later, the same tourists are ambushed. Men in military-style uniforms and gas masks, communicating via strange radio static, surround their camp. The commandos ignore all valuables. They dig with urgency. Within minutes, they uncover five steel boxes, load them onto their shoulders, and vanish back into the jungle. The police investigation is swift. They find the abandoned gold bar—real, 1kg of pure gold. They find the camp, the shallow holes. And they identify the vehicle used in the escape, registered to a man named Mohamad. He confesses to the whole plot: a simple gold heist from a nearby mine, using theatrical costumes to scare off witnesses. Case closed. The costumes go into an evidence room. Ten months later, a police officer on unrelated duty is shown a photo of the recovered gas mask. He freezes. He’d seen that exact mask before. It was part of a top-secret, now-declassified military inventory from a special unit, officially disbanded years prior. The unit’s known codename? Ghost. And their specialty? Psychological operations—using fear and disinformation as weapons. The gold was found. The perpetrators were named. But the mask in the evidence locker wasn't a cheap prop. It was genuine, surplus, and should not have existed. So who really wrote the script for that night in the jungle?
