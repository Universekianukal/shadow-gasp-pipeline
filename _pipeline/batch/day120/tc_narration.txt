June 1997. Indiana farmer Kevin Krug opens his front door to find his teenage son, Mark, pointing a .22 rifle at him. He shoots his father twice. Then Kevin’s wife, Louise, arrives home. She sees her husband’s body. Mark shoots her, too. He drags the bodies onto a wagon, hitches it to a tractor, and buries his parents in a shallow grave on the farm property. For weeks, Mark lives alone in the house with their car still in the drive. He collects his mother’s paychecks. He feeds their dog, Zeke. He keeps the secret from the older sister next door. The charade finally snaps when family members report Kevin and Louise missing. Police find the grave. Mark initially blames a supposed intruder. But the story falls apart. He confesses, telling police he murdered them for 'their bad values,' and for the house and land. A jury convicts him. He gets 120 years. And the question lingers: Why? They were his biggest supporters, described as loving parents. But in his confession, Mark states plainly: They loved him. And he hated them. A dark, fundamental disconnect at the heart of a family farm.
