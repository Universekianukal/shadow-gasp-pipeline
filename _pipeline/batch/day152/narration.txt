November 21, 2008. The sailing yacht LeMoyne Star issues a distress call. Position: 400 miles off the coast of Oregon. “Man overboard.” The owner, 42-year-old Kenneth LeMoyne, is missing. The Coast Guard airlifts his two crewmates to safety. They’re shaken, disoriented. Their story is clean: a rogue wave in rough seas, a failed safety harness. An accident. The $1.2 million vessel is towed to shore. Investigators find the life raft still in its canister. The EPIRB emergency beacon, untouched. And a single, glaring detail: the ship’s wheel was locked. Hard to starboard. Deliberately turned. This wasn't a wave. Somebody steered the boat on a sudden, sharp turn. The crew’s story collapses. They now claim LeMoyne jumped. That he'd been depressed. Police find no history. No note. Just two men on a locked boat with a missing boss and a new story. The FBI opens a murder investigation. A grand jury indicts both crewmen. One confesses. He says they drugged LeMoyne, bound him, and threw him into the sea. A clear case. But during the federal trial, the confession is recanted. The jury hears only circumstantial evidence. The steering wheel. The changing stories. After two days of deliberation, they return a verdict. Not guilty. Both men walk. The LeMoyne Star is sold. The case is closed. But one question lingers, echoing from that locked wheel: If he jumped, why did he turn the boat first?
