May 26, 1985. The steel-hulled tall ship MV Marques is racing through the Bermuda Triangle at 6 AM, her crew of 28 asleep below. Her four sister ships are sailing in formation. A rogue wave, an estimated 75-foot wall of water, strikes without warning. The Marques capsizes in 90 seconds. The crew of 28—mostly young British merchant navy cadets—is thrown into the Atlantic. By daylight, the four other ships can only recover three survivors, all clinging to debris. 25 are declared lost at sea. The official inquiry concludes that the unmodified design—originally built for the 1917 Baltic timber trade—was dangerously unstable for ocean racing. The vessel simply rolled over and sank. But a decade later, deep-sea explorers discover the wreck upright on the seafloor. Intact. Sealed hatches suggest no one tried to escape. The interior shows no signs of panic. 25 bodies are still inside, perfectly preserved in the cold darkness, exactly as they were when the wave hit. A final, silent, and unexplained act of discipline—or surrender.
