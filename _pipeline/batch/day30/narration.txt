April 1878. A miner climbs down Shaft Number Three at Cerro Gordo and finds the mine's bookkeeper, Louis D. Gordon, dead at the bottom — neck broken, ledger missing from his coat. Cerro Gordo shipped over $500,000 in silver-lead bullion in 1877 alone. Every bar stamped at 83 pounds, hauled by thirty-mule teams down the Yellow Grade Road to Owens Lake. Gordon logged each one before it left the mountain. By fall of '77 he'd found a pattern — bars running two pounds light, month after month. Fourteen months. Sixty bars gone. Roughly $18,000 in silver, half a million today. March 2nd, 1878: Gordon writes to the owners in San Francisco, demanding an audit. Two weeks later, he's dead. The coroner rules it an accidental fall — same day, no autopsy, case closed inside an hour. His ledger was never found. Within a month, shipments were back to full weight. No one was ever charged. In 1880 the record office burned to the ground, taking whatever books remained. The $18,000 was never recovered. Cerro Gordo still sits abandoned above the valley — and somewhere near Shaft Number Three, Gordon's ledger has never surfaced.
