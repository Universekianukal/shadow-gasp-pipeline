At 11:15 AM on August 16, 1942, a 250-foot Navy blimp drifts over a Daly City neighborhood — engines running, propellers spinning, radios crackling. It clips power lines, bounces off a roof, and slams into a house at 265 Bellevue Avenue. Bystanders rush the gondola. It's empty. No pilot. No copilot. No blood. No sign of struggle. The L-8 launched from Treasure Island at 6:03 AM with two men aboard — Lieutenant Ernest Cody, 27, and Ensign Charles Adams, 38. Orders: investigate a reported oil slick 25 miles west, near the Farallon Islands. At 7:42 AM, Cody radioed they'd found it. At 8:25 AM, a second transmission. Then silence. A fishing boat crew watched the blimp circle the slick at 30 feet, then climb back to altitude. Nobody saw anyone fall. Here's what breaks the case. Every piece of survival gear was still aboard. Both parachutes, strapped in their racks. The life raft, stowed. The flare gun, in its bracket. The safety line — the rope the crew clipped to themselves outside the gondola — hung loose, unclipped. The door was latched open. The Navy spent four months testing weight imbalances, enemy action, a fight between the crew, even a secret parachute jump. Every scenario left evidence unexplained. Cody and Adams were declared dead in 1943. No bodies. Ever. The L-8 was repaired and flew again. Crews refused to board her. They called her the ghost blimp.
