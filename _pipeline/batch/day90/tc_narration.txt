September 28, 1994, 1:15 AM. The MS Estonia is cutting through 15-meter waves in the Baltic Sea with 989 people aboard. A metallic bang. Then another. The bow visor — a 50-ton steel door at the ship's front — has torn free. Within 40 minutes, the ferry rolls 90 degrees. Water pours through open car deck ramps. 852 people die in 4-degree water. Only 137 survive. The 1997 joint investigation closes the case: faulty bow visor locks. Engineering failure. Done. But survivors reported explosions before the visor failed. The wreck was sealed under an international treaty — making it a crime to dive. Sweden classified underwater footage for years. Then in 2020, a documentary crew sends a remotely operated sub to the hull. They find a four-meter gash in the starboard side — damage no official report ever mentioned. A hole that experts say matches no known bow visor failure pattern. Sweden and Estonia reopen the investigation in 2021. Three years later, no published findings. The hole's origin remains unexplained. The wreck sits in 80 meters of freezing water, guarded by law, still holding whatever happened that night at the bottom of the Baltic.
