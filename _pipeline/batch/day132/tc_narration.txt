September 1st, 1923. The seismic shock of the Great Kanto Earthquake levels Tokyo and Yokohama in under five minutes. Chaos erupts in the rubble. Then, the rumor begins. It travels faster than any fire: Koreans are poisoning the wells. Koreans are looting. Koreans are rebelling. Vigilante militias form on the spot, arming themselves with bamboo spears, kendo swords, and military-issued rifles. Roadblocks appear. Suspects are dragged from homes and shelters. The questioning is a single word: 'Jūgo'. Say it. If your pronunciation of the Japanese number 15 isn't perfect, the sentence is execution. They're taken to the riverbanks, to vacant lots, behind police boxes. In Kameido, over 200 are massacred in a single night. The official death toll is given as 6,000, but Korean community records suggest tens of thousands. Then the twist. Tokyo police knew. They authorized the vigilante groups. Some officers even led the killing squads. Aftermath: a state-sponsored cover-up. The 1924 government report downplays the numbers, blames 'mass hysteria'. No one is prosecuted. No memorials are built. The truth becomes a ghost, whispered but unacknowledged. The open wound remains: how does a modern nation erase a premeditated massacre from its own ground, and from its people's memory?
