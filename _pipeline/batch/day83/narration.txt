January 23, 2013. A 300-foot cruise liner snaps free of its tow line 50 nautical miles off Newfoundland. The MV Lyubov Orlova — a Russian vessel seized in St. John's harbor over $795,000 in unpaid debts, left to rot for nearly two years. Chained to the dock. Auctioned for scrap. Worth almost nothing. A tug hauls it toward the Dominican Republic — 1,400 nautical miles to the breakers' yard. In heavy seas, the cable snaps. The tug sails on. Nobody comes back. Transport Canada writes it off. Not worth the cost of recovery. The ship drifts into international waters. No crew. No power. No lights. 4,000 tons of steel, moving with the current. February 2013. US satellite radar detects a massive object drifting toward Iceland. The Irish coast guard prepares for impact. Then the signal vanishes. No further contact. No wreckage. No distress beacon. Weeks later, a lifeboat washes ashore in Ireland. Serial numbers confirmed — it belonged to the Lyubov Orlova. But the ship itself? Nothing. Every sonar sweep. Nothing. Fishermen still report a dark shape drifting without lights. Marine insurers declared it a total loss. Presumed sunk. But no wreck has ever been found. 4,000 tons of steel, somewhere in the Atlantic. Or nowhere. Still missing.
