On June 18, 1982, at 7:30 AM, a postal clerk crossing Blackfriars Bridge in London spots a body hanging from scaffolding beneath the arches. The man wears a suit. His pockets are stuffed with bricks and $14,000 in cash across three currencies. His name is Roberto Calvi — chairman of Banco Ambrosiano, known to the world as God's Banker. Days earlier, his bank collapsed with $1.3 billion missing, much of it routed through Vatican shell companies and a secret Masonic lodge called P2. Calvi had fled Rome on a false passport — Venice first, then London. Now he's dead under a bridge whose name, Blackfriars, echoes a shadow history all its own. Police rule it suicide within 48 hours. But the details don't fit. The bricks in his pockets don't match any rubble found near the bridge. The water in his lungs doesn't match the river beneath him. His shoes are clean — impossible for a man who climbed down to that scaffolding alive. Twenty years later, a second forensic review overturns the verdict: unlawful killing. In 2005, five defendants stand trial in Rome for his murder. All five are acquitted. The case goes cold again. Who killed God's Banker — and why the bricks, why that bridge — remains unanswered.
