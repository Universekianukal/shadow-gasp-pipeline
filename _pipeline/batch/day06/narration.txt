October 14th, 1941. German soldiers pry six tons of gold-backed amber off palace walls in under 36 hours. What they're stealing took Prussian craftsmen a decade to build back in 1701 — a gift for Peter the Great, worth today an estimated $500 million. They ship it 700 miles to Königsberg Castle. For three years, Nazi officials display it like a trophy. Then, August 1944 — British bombers hit the castle. Ash and rubble everywhere. Official story: the Amber Room burned to nothing. Except witnesses swear they saw crates loaded onto a train weeks after the fire. The castle's curator, Alfred Rohde, tells investigators three different stories in three different years. In 1945, Soviet agents arrest him for questioning. Within days, he's dead — officially typhus. No autopsy photos survive. Germany rebuilt a replica in 2003, using $11 million and old photographs. It sits in Russia now, gleaming, fake. The real one? Never found. Treasure hunters have drilled through Baltic mines, searched sunken ships, dug up bunkers for eighty years. Nothing. Somewhere — a bunker, a lakebed, a wall — six tons of amber might still be waiting.
