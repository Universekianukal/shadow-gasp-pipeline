On May 4, 1932, a Stockholm landlord forces open the door to apartment 4 on Sankt Eriksplan and finds Lilly Lindeström lying on her bed, dead. She is 32. She has been bludgeoned with a blunt object. But that is not what freezes the responding officers. Her body has been almost entirely drained of blood. Not pooled beneath her. Not splashed across the room. Gone. On a saucer beside the bed sits a ladle, coated in congealed blood. The murder weapon — likely a small hammer — is never recovered. A used condom remains on Lilly's body, placing a client in the apartment within hours of death. Neighbors on the floor heard nothing. No struggle. No scream. Police interview every known associate. Nothing sticks. A bloodstained coat is recovered but cannot be traced to anyone. The ladle earns the case its press name: the Atlas Vampire, after the neighborhood. Ninety-two years later, the evidence box sits in Stockholm police archives. The ladle is still inside. The case file is still open. No suspect was ever named. No DNA was ever recovered. The blood was never accounted for.
