October 25, 1918. A 2,200-ton steamship sits stranded on a reef in Alaska's Lynn Canal. For forty hours, it hasn't moved. Rescuers wait in boats just yards away. The captain has assured them — the ship is secure. The SS Princess Sophia ran aground on Vanderbilt Reef on October 23, at 2:10 AM. The reef sat six miles from shore, invisible beneath the high tide. Captain Leonard Locke sent a distress call. Within hours, fishing boats, a lighthouse tender, another CPR steamer — all gathered around the wreck. The ship listed. But it held. Passengers waited. Rescuers waited. The water was too rough to board, and the captain believed evacuation was more dangerous than staying put. At 5:30 PM on October 25, the wireless operator sent his final message. The ship was taking water. It was foundering. Then — silence. When rescuers reached the reef at dawn, the Sophia was gone. Swept off completely. Not one survivor. Over 360 people — passengers, crew, children. Bodies and personal effects washed ashore for weeks along sixty miles of coastline. The passenger manifest went down with the ship. To this day, no one has confirmed the exact number of dead. The sea never returned its count.
