February 11th, 1893. The S.S. Naronic, a brand-new White Star Line cattle carrier, vanishes in the mid-Atlantic. No distress call. No wreckage. Just a single clue: two lifeboats, found empty, adrift and separated by 200 miles. The official inquiry blames an iceberg. Then, over six decades later, another mystery resurfaces. In 1956, a man named James H. Winchester investigates a bizarre story. A retired sea captain confesses on his deathbed. He claims he saw the Naronic’s lifeboats not in 1893, but in 1912—nineteen years after the ship was lost. The captain describes them moored together in a secluded Alaskan cove. He counted twelve boats, all bearing the White Star Line flag. The paint was fresh. The oars were stowed. They were perfectly seaworthy, yet utterly abandoned. Winchester searches for records, for the cove, for the captain’s identity. He finds nothing. No logbook entries. No corroborating witnesses. No trace of twelve lifeboats that shouldn't exist. The official case closed in 1893. The real story, if the dying captain told the truth, only began decades later. So where did those boats come from? And where did they go? Two separate vanishings, connected only by a deathbed whisper and twelve ghostly, impossible vessels.
