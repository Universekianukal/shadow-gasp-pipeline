6,000 sheep lay dead across 30 miles of Utah desert in a single night. March 14, 1968. Skull Valley. The animals had been grazing normally the evening before. By morning, they were staggering, convulsing, dropping in rows. No wounds. No predator. No disease any veterinarian could identify. Three miles west sat Dugway Proving Ground — the Army's primary chemical weapons test site. The night before, March 13, Dugway conducted an open-air test of a chemical agent. The wind was blowing east. Straight toward the grazing herds. Veterinarians found symptoms matching organophosphate nerve agent poisoning. Pinpoint pupils. Convulsions. Respiratory collapse. The same symptoms caused by VX — a weapon so lethal that a single drop on skin kills in minutes. The Army denied responsibility for two years. Then quietly paid ranchers nearly one million dollars. But the official report never named VX. Never explained how the agent traveled three miles. And never tested the ranchers who handled the dying sheep — bare-handed, for days. Six thousand animals dead. Not one human studied. The Skull Valley files remain partially classified.
