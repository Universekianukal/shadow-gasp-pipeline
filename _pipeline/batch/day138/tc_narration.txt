On November 23, 1976, a graduate student walks into a Georgia Tech research lab. He’s there to work with a depleted uranium sphere. It is heavy, fist-sized, valuable—and gone. The sphere vanished from a padlocked cabinet, behind a locked lab door. The material is depleted, not weapons-grade, but it is still highly radioactive—and lethally toxic if mishandled. Campus police launch a search. They scan the building with Geiger counters. Nothing. They widen the search. The sphere has a half-life of 4.5 billion years, but its trail went cold in hours. The investigation focuses on student labs, scrap thieves, even radical environmental groups. Every lead dissolves. Here’s the reversal: two years later, a strange pattern emerges. Mysterious letters arrive at local newspapers signed 'The Uranium Committee,' claiming responsibility. They detail how and why the theft was committed. But the details are wrong—scientifically impossible. Officials declare the letters a hoax. The sphere was never recovered. No arrests were ever made. Did a student take it as a macabre trophy? Was it sold for scrap and melted down, its poison released? Or does it still sit in a box in someone’s attic, a silent, deadly relic of a perfect, unsolved heist?
