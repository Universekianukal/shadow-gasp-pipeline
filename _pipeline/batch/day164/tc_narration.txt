December 2nd, 1870. The British clipper Sappho, 1,100 tons, clears the River Mersey at Liverpool. She carries 28 souls and a cargo of salt. Her destination is Melbourne, Australia. Nothing unusual. Nothing foreboding. By late December, the Sappho should be deep in the Atlantic, making good speed. The ocean is empty. For months, British shipping lines post anxious inquiries across maritime hubs. No wreckage is reported. No survivors wash ashore. Then, in February 1871, the barque Lady of the Lake reports sighting a three-masted ship. The vessel is completely dismasted, adrift, and flying a distress flag. The Lady of the Lake's captain notes the longitude and the bearing. The hulk's description matches the Sappho. But a fierce gale prevents any approach. The barque is forced to sail on, logging the sighting with grim finality. Here is the reversal: The Royal Navy immediately dispatches a search vessel to that exact Atlantic grid. They find nothing. No floating timbers. No cargo. Not a single clue. The Sappho, a modern ship on a well-traveled route, leaves behind a final, ghostly sighting and then dissolves. Its official fate is simply 'posted missing.' Modern theories range from catastrophic structural failure to rogue waves. But the core fact remains. In the age of steam and telegraph, with thousands of transatlantic crossings logged, a major ship vanished after being seen adrift, and the rescue mission found an empty sea. Where did the hulk go?
