June 1866. A 27-stone butcher from Wagga Wagga steps off a ship in London and announces: I am Sir Roger Tichborne. Roger drowned in 1854 when the Bella sank off Brazil. He was 25, slim, fluent in French, with a distinctive tattoo on his left arm. This man weighs nearly 400 pounds. No tattoo. Can't speak a word of French. Doesn't recognize the horse he supposedly rode for years. But Lady Henriette Tichborne, twelve years into grief, looks at him once and says: 'This is my son.' She grants him £1,000 a year. Servants who knew Roger for decades call it impossible. Lawyers investigate. They say his real name is Arthur Orton, a butcher's son from Wapping, East London. In 1871 a civil trial opens. It runs 103 days — the longest in English legal history to that date. He loses. He's arrested for perjury before he leaves the courtroom. The second trial runs 188 days. Guilty. Fourteen years' hard labor. He serves ten, then sells a confession to a newspaper for cash — and recants it four days later. He dies in 1898, still signing letters 'Sir Roger Tichborne.' His coffin plate carries the name he went to prison for stealing.
