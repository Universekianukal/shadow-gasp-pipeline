March 2009. A burned body in eastern France. Investigators run the DNA. The match: a profile linked to over forty crime scenes across three countries. One problem — the DNA belongs to a woman. The corpse is male. For sixteen years, from 1993 to 2009, this same female DNA turned up everywhere. A stolen car in Germany. A heroin syringe in Austria. A burglarized caravan. A child's toy gun. Police called her the Phantom of Heilbronn — Europe's most wanted woman, linked to six murders, a 300,000-euro bounty on her head. On April 25th, 2007, in Heilbronn, her DNA showed up on a policewoman's uniform. Michèle Kiesewetter, twenty-two years old, shot once through the head at point-blank range. But the phantom never existed. Investigators traced the DNA to a single factory in Austria. The cotton swabs used to collect crime-scene samples had been contaminated on the production line — by a female factory worker. Every case, unconnected. Sixteen years of false leads gone in a week. But Kiesewetter's killer was real. Her murder is still unsolved. Her service weapon later surfaced hundreds of miles away — in the ashes of a neo-Nazi hideout.
