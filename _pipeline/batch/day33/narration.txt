January 15th, 1919. Boston's North End. A rivet pops on a five-story steel tank and 2.3 million gallons of molasses erupt into the street. The wave is 25 feet tall, moving at 35 miles an hour. It flattens buildings. It lifts a firehouse off its foundation. Horses drown standing up. Twenty-one people die. A hundred and fifty are injured. Some victims aren't found for days, buried under hardened sugar. The company, United States Industrial Alcohol, has an explanation ready: anarchists bombed the tank. Investigators dig instead into the tank itself. It was never tested with water. It was painted brown to hide leaks workers had reported for months. Neighborhood kids used to collect leaking molasses in buckets for candy. The lawsuit drags six years. In 1925, USIA finally pays: $628,000, one of the largest settlements in Massachusetts history. The harbor stayed brown until summer. Cleanup crews used saltwater and sand for weeks and still couldn't get it all. To this day, residents swear that on hot August afternoons, if the wind is right, the North End still smells faintly, unmistakably sweet.
