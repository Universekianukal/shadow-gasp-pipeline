Frau Troffea steps into a narrow Strasbourg street on July 14, 1518, and starts to dance. No music plays. She doesn't stop. Not that hour, not that night. By day four she's still moving, feet bleeding through her shoes. Neighbors think she's possessed. Within a week, 34 more join her, dancing without rest, without reason. Physicians examine them and rule out demons — they diagnose "hot blood" and prescribe more dancing. The city council agrees. They build a wooden stage. They hire musicians. They clear two guildhalls for a dance floor. By August, over 400 people move to fiddles and drums until their bodies give out. Some accounts report 15 deaths a day — strokes, heart attacks, sheer exhaustion, dancers collapsing mid-step. The cure isn't stopping them. It's giving them a stage. By September, survivors are hauled to a mountain shrine and the dancing simply ends. No one is ever punished, blamed, or explained. Modern theories point to ergot fungus, mass stress, religious hysteria — but Strasbourg's own records describe something closer to a trance no one could break. Four hundred people danced themselves toward death, and five centuries later, nobody knows what pulled them into the street.
