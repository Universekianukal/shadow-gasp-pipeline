A Boeing 727 accelerates down the runway at Luanda Airport, Angola. No flight plan. No clearance. No transponder signal. It's May 25, 2003 — two years after 9/11 — and a 100,000-pound commercial jetliner is stealing itself. The tower calls every frequency. Static. The aircraft lifts off over the Atlantic and vanishes from radar. Investigators believe two men were aboard: Ben Charles Padilla, an American flight mechanic, and John Mikel Mutantu, a Congolese assistant. Neither held a pilot's license. The jet, tail number N844AA, had sat derelict on the tarmac for 14 months — engines removed, tires flat, owned by a shell company with a mailing address nobody could verify. The FBI, CIA, NATO, and allied intelligence across four continents launched the largest search for a vehicle in history. Satellites swept West Africa. Nothing. Then the reversal. The 727 was worth more dismantled than airworthy — its parts valued at nearly $400,000. Insurance records revealed six ownership disputes across four countries in two years. Multiple parties collected when it simply disappeared. No wreckage. No bodies. No ransom. No terror attack. Twenty-two years later, N844AA is the only commercial airliner in history never found.
