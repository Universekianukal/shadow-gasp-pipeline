A British forest guard crests a ridge at 16,500 feet in the Indian Himalayas and freezes. Skeletons. Hundreds of them. Ringing the edge of a frozen lake so remote it has no name on any map. The year is 1942. The skulls are pristine, bleached white by altitude and sun. No clothing survives. No weapons. No documentation. Just bone — piled in shallow water, frozen into the gravel. Initial count: roughly 200 individuals. A World War II subcommittee wonders: are these Japanese soldiers who died of exposure on a secret invasion route? Geologists say no. The bone is far too old. For seventy years, the leading theory is a single catastrophe — a ninth-century pilgrimage struck down by a hailstorm so violent the bodies were never recovered. Folk songs from the region even mention a goddess who rained stones on disrespectful travelers. Then 2019. Researchers extract DNA from 38 skeletons. The results break the case wide open — and make it worse. The dead are not one group. They are not from the same century. Some share ancestry with present-day South Asians. Others match the Mediterranean. A third cluster traces to Southeast Asia. The skeletons span roughly a thousand years. No mass catastrophe explains that. No single hailstorm. No one event. Roopkund was not a tomb for one disaster. It was a graveyard for dozens of them — and nobody knows why this specific lake, this specific ridge, drew people to die across an entire millennium.
