Three Mounties stand on a frozen porch in the Arctic dark. Behind the door, a man they've never identified opens fire through the wood. Constable Alfred King drops, hit. It is December 31, 1931, 40 below zero, and the siege of Rat River has just begun. The shooter called himself Albert Johnson. He arrived in Fort McPherson six months earlier — no known history, no fingerprints on file, no family. He built a cabin 80 miles from the nearest settlement and started tampering with Indigenous traplines. After the shooting, a nine-man posse returned on January 30, 1932. Fifteen-hour siege. They dynamited the cabin. Johnson clawed out of the rubble and vanished into the barrens — wounded, on foot, at minus 40. The manhunt stretched 150 miles. The RCMP flew in a bush plane — almost unheard of in the Arctic that year. Constable Edgar Millen caught Johnson on February 1 and was shot dead. Johnson kept running two more weeks through terrain that should have killed any man. On February 17, they cornered him at Eagle River. He died shooting. But here's what doesn't add up. No identification. Dental records matched nobody. Fingerprints returned nothing. A trapper who shot like a soldier and survived conditions that killed experienced men — and no one ever learned who he was. His grave in Aklavik still reads Albert Johnson. That was never his name.
