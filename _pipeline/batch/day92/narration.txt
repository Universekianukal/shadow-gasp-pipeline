June 1985. A woman lies on a table at Kennestone Regional Oncology Center in Marietta, Georgia. The Therac-25 fires. She screams — a tremendous flash of light, a burning sensation. The operator sees "Malfunction 54" on screen. No explanation. No warning that the patient just absorbed 100 times the prescribed dose. The operator re-runs the treatment. Over 18 months, six patients are blasted with massive overdoses across Georgia, Texas, Ontario, and Washington. Three die. The Therac-25 was supposed to be safer than its predecessors. AECL had removed the hardware safety interlocks that existed in older models. Everything depended on software — software never independently reviewed. When investigators traced the bug, it was a race condition. Edit the prescription fast enough and the machine fired its high-energy beam without the metal flattening filter. A single keystroke's timing. That was the difference between therapy and a lethal dose. "Malfunction 54" told operators nothing. So they hit retry. AECL insisted the machine was safe. They kept insisting — for two years — while patients kept burning.
