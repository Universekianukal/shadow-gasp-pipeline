February 15, 2003. Antwerp's Diamond District. A vault two floors underground, sealed behind ten layers of security — motion detectors, seismic sensors, a magnetic field, a lock with one hundred million combinations. By Monday morning, one hundred twenty-three of its one hundred eighty-nine safety deposit boxes sit empty. Gone: over one hundred million dollars in diamonds, gold, and jewels. Zero alarms triggered. The thieves — a five-man crew Belgian press nicknamed the School of Turin — spent over a year studying guard shifts, cloning keys, and defeating the vault's own sensors with foam, tape, and ordinary hairspray to fool a light-based detector. Police have nothing. No prints. No usable footage. Then, three days later, a forest ranger near Lier finds trash dumped along a roadside — torn evidence bags, gloves, a videotape — and a half-eaten salami sandwich. DNA on the crust matches Leonardo Notarbartolo, the crew's inside man, who'd rented a shop inside the Diamond Center for years just to earn the building's trust. He's caught. He serves ten years. But Notarbartolo has always insisted he wasn't the mastermind — that a man he calls only "Mr. Big" financed and planned it all, then disappeared. Ninety percent of the diamonds were never found.
