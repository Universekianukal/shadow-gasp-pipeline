A 255-foot schooner sits hard aground on Diamond Shoals, sails still set, wheel locked — and not a single soul aboard. January 31, 1921. The Carroll A. Deering, a five-masted cargo schooner, spotted from the Cape Hatteras light station, listing in heavy surf. Rough seas kept rescuers away for four days. When the Coast Guard finally boarded on February 4, the galley table was set for a meal — food laid out, pots still warm. Eleven men had simply walked off. The lifeboats were gone. So were the anchors, the chronometer, the charts, the ship's log, and every personal belonging. But nothing was ransacked. The cargo of coal sat untouched. No sign of struggle, no blood, no damage beyond what the reef had done. This wasn't piracy. Someone had methodically stripped the Deering of everything needed to navigate — then left the ship sailing under no one's command. Three separate ships' crews reported seeing a mysterious steamer hovering near the Deering on January 28, the last day anyone made contact. The FBI opened one of its first federal investigations. They chased pirates, mutineers, Bolsheviks. Six months later, a bottle washed ashore with a note claiming the Deering was captured. The note was traced to a known forager — a hoax. The eleven men were never found. No bodies. No lifeboats. No explanation.
