December 4th, 1872. Four hundred miles east of the Azores. The crew of the Dei Gratia spots something wrong — a ship drifting, sails half-set, no one at the wheel. They board. Her name: Mary Celeste. Captain Benjamin Briggs. His wife Sarah. Their two-year-old daughter, Sophia. Seven crewmen. All gone. The last log entry: November 25th — nine days earlier, three hundred miles back. No storm damage. No blood. No struggle. The lifeboat — missing. Six months of food and water, still stocked below deck, untouched. Sixteen hundred barrels of alcohol in the hold — nine empty, the rest intact. Investigators expected mutiny. Piracy. Insurance fraud. They found none of it — just a ship in near-perfect order, abandoned mid-sentence, as if everyone stepped outside for air and never came back in. No bodies. No wreckage. No distress call. Ten people vanished from a floating, seaworthy vessel in open ocean — and to this day, not one has ever been found. The Mary Celeste sailed on for thirteen more years. Every captain after her called her cursed.
