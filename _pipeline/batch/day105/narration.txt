On July 25, 1978, a basement wall in Celle, West Germany begins to crack open. Behind it: not a neighbor's storage room — a 40-meter tunnel, timber-lined, ventilated by electric fans, lit by working bulbs, leading to a locked door across the street. The tunnel is 1.6 meters high. Professional grade. Someone spent weeks digging it. The target was an apartment suspected of housing Red Army Faction sympathizers. West Germany's domestic intelligence service, the Verfassungsschutz, ordered the dig to plant listening devices. But the agents broke through into the wrong basement. The tenant calls police. The Verfassungsschutz denies everything. For eight years the agency blames criminals, then foreign agents. In 1986 a whistleblower confirms the truth. The scandal forces resignations. The operation cost over 300,000 deutschmarks and produced zero usable intelligence. What was never answered: the agency's files on the operation were ordered destroyed. Who gave that order — and whether other tunnels were dug beneath other West German cities — remains classified to this day.
