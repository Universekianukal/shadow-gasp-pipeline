April 1872: Philip Arnold walks into the Bank of California and dumps a canvas sack onto the president's desk. Diamonds spill out. The biggest assayers in San Francisco value them at $1.5 million. Arnold and his partner, John Slack, take a $50,000 advance for their 'secret diamond field' in Wyoming Territory. They lead two expert investors, General David Colton and Charles Tiffany himself, on a staged expedition. The men find gems scattered across the ground. The investors wire $650,000 for full ownership. The total investment hits two million - over fifty million today. But the 'mine' was salted. The 'diamonds' were industrial-grade stones and crushed common glass, planted months earlier. The investors, including the richest men in America, were left with nothing but bad land. Arnold fled to Kentucky and used his loot to build a bank. He was later shot in the back by a debtor, but never served a day for the scheme. The 'diamond field' was worthless rock. The mystery is who, besides the two grifters, knew about the fraud. Court records suggest someone far higher up, perhaps inside those very banks, knew the stones were being appraised at wildly inflated values. The money vanished. The true orchestrators were never named.
