Marylebone, London. A Sunday night, September 1971. Inside a rented handbag shop two doors from Lloyds Bank, a gang has been digging for three days. Forty feet of tunnel. Straight under a Chicken Inn restaurant. Straight into the vault. At 1:15 a.m., a thermic lance burns through three feet of reinforced concrete. Sparks. Smoke. Then — silence, and cash. Across town, an amateur radio operator named Robert Rowlands is scanning frequencies. He hears a voice: 'The whole bloody place will fall down.' Then coordinates. He calls police. Twice. Officers search. They can't find the bank. Nobody gave them an address. The robbery runs uninterrupted for two more hours. By Monday morning, 268 safe deposit boxes sit open. Between £1.5 and £3 million gone — cash, jewels, documents. Four men are eventually convicted. The mastermind is never named. Most of the money is never found. Then the real oddity surfaces: a government D-notice, quietly issued, blocking press coverage of the case for years. Officially, national security. Unofficially? Rumors of compromising photographs inside one particular box — never confirmed, never denied. Nobody has ever said whose box it was.
