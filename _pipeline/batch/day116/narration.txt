July 17, 1944. 10:18 PM. Port Chicago, California. Two cargo ships packed with 4,600 tons of munitions detonate in a single blast felt 50 miles away. 320 men die instantly. 202 of them are Black sailors — assigned to load explosives with zero training and no safety equipment. The blast vaporizes both vessels, flattens the pier, and leaves a crater 66 feet wide and 20 feet deep in the bay floor. Three weeks later, surviving Black sailors are ordered back to loading ammunition at a neighboring depot. Same cargo. Same ships. Same nonexistent training. 258 men refuse. The Navy calls it mutiny. 50 are charged and tried in a closed court-martial. Their military defense attorney calls zero witnesses. Each verdict takes roughly 36 minutes. All 50 convicted. Fifteen years of hard labor. A young Thurgood Marshall sits through the trial, files appeals, publishes a scathing report calling the proceedings a sham. The convictions stand. In 1999, one survivor receives a presidential pardon. The other 49 convictions have never been overturned. The Navy has never formally apologized.
