New Year's morning, 1963. A physicist and his mistress lie dead beside a Sydney riverbank. No wounds. No marks. No weapon. Dr. Gilbert Bogle, 38 — a CSIRO physicist working on classified optics research. Margaret Chandler, 29, mother of three. Both married to other people. Geoffrey Chandler, Margaret's husband, discovered them. He'd known about the affair. He'd even lent Bogle his car that night. Police tested for every known poison. Arsenic. Cyanide. Strychnine. All negative. The autopsy found something unidentified in Bogle's stomach. The coroner returned an open finding. Cause of death: unknown. Then it got stranger. Bogle's classified work drew ASIO — Australia's intelligence agency — into the case. Files vanished. A clerk was told to stop asking questions. For 60 years, theories multiplied. Mushroom poisoning. LSD, which CSIRO was quietly studying. A jealous husband's revenge. In 2020, a researcher proposed hydrogen sulfide gas from the polluted Lane Cove River — colorless, lethal, leaving no trace. It fits. But no one tested for it in 1963. The files are still sealed.
