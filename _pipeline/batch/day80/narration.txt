August 8, 1975. 1:07 AM. A wall of water six meters high and ten kilometers wide tears through Henan Province, erasing 600,000 homes in under six hours. It comes from the Banqiao Dam — a structure engineers had called "the iron dam," built to survive a once-in-a-thousand-year flood. But that weekend, Typhoon Nina dropped 1,060 millimeters of rain in a single day. A once-in-two-thousand-year event. The reservoir rose two meters above the dam's crest. Operators radioed for permission to open the spillway gates. Permission never came. At 1:07 AM, the dam shattered. 700 million cubic meters of water surged downstream at 50 kilometers per hour. Shimantan Dam collapsed 30 minutes later. Then another. Then another. Sixty-two dams fell in a chain reaction across the Ruhe River basin. The official death toll was 26,000. But that number counted only the immediate flood. Disease, starvation, and exposure pushed real estimates past 230,000. China didn't acknowledge the disaster publicly for 18 years. The meteorological records were sealed. The exact death count remains unknown — and the rebuilt Banqiao Dam stands 16 meters taller, holding back the same valley.
