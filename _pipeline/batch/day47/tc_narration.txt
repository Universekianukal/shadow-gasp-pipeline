Bedford County, Virginia, January 1820. A stranger named Thomas Beale checks into the Washington Hotel with two locked iron boxes and a story nobody can verify. He hands one box to innkeeper Robert Morriss. Inside, he says, are papers written in cipher — worthless, unless he doesn't return within ten years. He rides out. He never comes back. Morriss waits. Not ten years — twenty-three. In 1845 he finally breaks the lock. Three sheets of numbers stare back at him. No key. No explanation. For decades the numbers mean nothing. Then in 1885 someone cracks cipher number two — using the Declaration of Independence as the key. It describes a vault four miles from Buford's Tavern: 2,921 pounds of gold. 5,100 pounds of silver. Jewels worth $13,000. At today's value, over $60 million. Cipher one — the location. Cipher three — the names of the men owed a share. Both remain unbroken. A century and a half of codebreakers, including wartime cryptanalysts, have failed. And here's the turn: many now believe Beale never existed at all — that the whole thing was invented by the man who published the pamphlet, to sell it for fifty cents a copy. No treasure has ever been dug up. Bedford County's hillsides are still pocked with holes. Nobody has stopped looking.
