It was the only theft in the Kunsthistorisches Museum's hundred-year history. May 11th, 1997. The museum's main hall stands silent. On the security video, a figure in black scales a construction scaffold. They smash a window. They bypass the alarm by disconnecting a single wire at the control panel—an inside job. They bypass seventeen other sensors. The target is a tiny, glass-topped pedestal. Inside rests the Saliera, a solid gold salt cellar, a national treasure. It weighs ten pounds, stands ten inches tall, crafted by Cellini in 1543. It’s valued at 60 million dollars. They vanish into the night rain. For three years, no leads, no ransom demand. Then, a flurry of letters, demanding a ransom of 100-million Austrian schillings. The police arrange a drop. The package arrives. Inside? Not the Saliera. A single, mocking note. Two more years pass. In January 2003, an anonymous tip leads police to a forest ninety kilometers north of Vienna. They find a metal box, buried beneath a tree. The lid is pried open. The golden cellar gleams inside, unharmed. The thief? Never caught. The inside man? Never identified. The wire they disconnected at the panel? The museum's chief of security was never investigated. He retired three months later. No one knows who stole it, how they planned it from the inside, or why they truly returned it after six years.
