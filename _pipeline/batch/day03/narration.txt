December 1st, 1948. Somerton Beach, Adelaide. A man in a suit lies against the seawall, legs crossed, like he's dozing in the sun. He's not. Passersby think he's drunk. He's already dead. No wallet. No ID. Every clothing label cut out — surgically, deliberately. In his pocket: a bus ticket, a comb, an unused rail ticket to Sydney. The autopsy finds no poison, no injuries, no cause of death — just an oddly shrunken spleen. Months later, a scrap of paper turns up, sewn into a hidden pocket in his trousers. Two words in Persian: Tamam Shud. 'Finished.' Torn from a rare edition of the Rubaiyat of Omar Khayyam. Police track down the actual book — tossed into an unlocked car nearby. Inside: a phone number, and five lines of scrawled capital letters. A code. Still unbroken. The number belongs to a nurse named Jestyn. Shown the book, she goes pale. She says she's never seen him before. In 2022, DNA finally gives him a name: Carl Webb, an engineer from Melbourne. But no one knows why he was in Adelaide. Why the disguise. Why the code. Seventy-five years on, the cipher still sits there, unsolved.
