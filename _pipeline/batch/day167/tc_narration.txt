January 21, 1968. A B-52 carrying four hydrogen bombs explodes over the ice near Thule Air Base, Greenland. The U.S. and Denmark launch a frantic, brutal cleanup — thousands of men, hundreds of flights, nine months to recover 93% of the plutonium. The official story ends there. Until 1971. Danish journalist Villy Nielsen uncovers a Pentagon report revealing a hidden crisis. During the cleanup, they lost an entire bomb. Not physically. Its 52-megaton thermonuclear stage — the 'secondary' — simply... disintegrated. Vaporized on impact. 1.1 kilograms of weapons-grade plutonium and an undisclosed quantity of highly enriched uranium were pulverized into fine, radioactive dust and scattered across the frozen bay, contaminating an estimated 140,000 cubic meters of ice. This is Thulegate. The Danish parliament, NATO's closest ally, was never told. For three years, Danish workers at the site, wearing only basic protective gear, unknowingly handled irradiated debris. Then, in 1974, a classified cable surfaces. A secret agreement between Copenhagen and Washington that had been in place since 1957. A "nuclear umbrella" pact that explicitly banned nuclear weapons over Danish soil, knowingly violated for over a decade. The bombs should never have been there. The cleanup was a lie. And the radiation? Still present. Because you can't clean up an aerosolized nuclear secret.
