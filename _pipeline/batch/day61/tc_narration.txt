A 14-month-old infant vanishes from a Versailles hospital ward in 1793. The surgeon finds a patient named Tarrare standing over the empty crib, blood on his hands and mouth. Tarrare was 21. He weighed roughly 100 pounds. He could eat a quarter of a cow in one sitting. His jaw stretched so wide his teeth barely met. He swallowed eels whole. Live cats. Stones. Cork. Military surgeon Pierre-François Percy documented everything. He thought Tarrare could work as a living courier — swallowing sealed intelligence documents, retrieving them intact days later. In 1792, Prussian forces captured him on his first mission. They whipped him, searched him, found nothing. The papers were still inside him. When the Prussians realized, they released him in disgust rather than cut him open. Here's the reversal. After the infant vanished, Tarrare didn't flee. He begged Percy to confine him. He said he couldn't stop. Percy chained him to a bed in a locked cell. Within days, Tarrare broke free, crawled through hospital drains, and disappeared. He surfaced in a Paris hospital five years later, dying. The autopsy found a stomach twelve times normal size, riddled with ulcers, reeking of pus. His liver and gallbladder were grossly enlarged. The cause of death was never determined. The infant's remains were never found.
