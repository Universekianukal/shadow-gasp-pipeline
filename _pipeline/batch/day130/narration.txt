11:29 a.m., August 12, 2000. A magnitude 1.5 blast tears through the K‑141 Kursk. The navy hears frantic signals. Two minutes later, silence.
For three days, Russia refuses international rescue. Divers reach the wreck. They film the mangled escape hatch. Behind it, the pressure hull is intact. Survivors could be inside.
Officially: a training torpedo accident. Closed case.
Independent analysts get the same sonar maps. Their conclusion is different. The blast pattern points to a live warhead detonating in a forward tube. Not a training propellant.
Most of the 118 crew died instantly. But a note, water-stained and folded, was recovered from a sailor's pocket. '13:15. It’s too dark to write... It seems there are no chances.'
They waited in the dark, cold compartment. A rescue filmed the hatch, then left.
The billion-dollar salvage operation cut away and sealed the entire forward section. It sits in a secure facility. Classified.
The hatch they filmed is rusting in a warehouse. The question of what exploded in Tube 4 remains.
