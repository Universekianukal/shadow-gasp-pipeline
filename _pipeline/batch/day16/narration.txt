May 23, 1918. New Orleans grocer Joseph Maggio and his wife Catherine are found in bed, skulls split by their own axe. A panel is chiseled clean out of their back door — no broken lock, no noise. Over the next ten months, six more households fall the same way. Always Italian grocers. Always a chiseled door panel. Always the weapon left behind, wiped clean. March 13, 1919: the Cortimiglia family. Two-year-old Mary is killed. Her parents are struck down in their own kitchen. Rosie survives and names two neighbors. Both are convicted. Then a letter reaches the Times-Picayune, signed 'the Axeman.' It claims to be a demon from hell. It promises to spare any house playing jazz at 12:15 a.m. on March 19. That night, New Orleans becomes the loudest city in America. The murders don't stop. Mike Pepitone dies that October — the last confirmed victim. Rosie recants. Her neighbors walk free. December 1920: Pepitone's widow shoots a man dead in Los Angeles, swearing he's the Axeman. No fingerprint. No confession. No trial ever proves it. Nobody knows who wrote that letter. Nobody ever will.
