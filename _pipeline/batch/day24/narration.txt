Yale's Beinecke Library keeps it under glass: 234 pages, written in a script no living person can read. Carbon dating places the vellum between 1404 and 1438 — the material is genuine, not a modern forgery. Every page is illustrated. Plants that match no known species. Figures bathing in green liquid, linked by strange tubes. A zodiac wheel labeled in symbols no one has decoded. In 1912, book dealer Wilfrid Voynich bought it from a Jesuit college in Italy and dragged it into the modern age. Codebreakers cracked Enigma in World War II. They failed here. The NSA tried in the 1970s. Failed too. Statisticians ran the letters through every known cipher and language pattern — and found something strange. The text behaves like real language. Not random noise. That's the twist: it's not nonsense. Something structured is locked inside it. A 1666 letter claims Emperor Rudolf II paid 600 gold ducats for it, believing it held the secrets of Roger Bacon. Unconfirmed. No author identified. No language identified. Not one full sentence translated in over a hundred years. The book sits in Connecticut, still saying nothing.
