November 29, 1781. Aboard the slave ship Zong, sailors seize the first of them — chained, terrified — and hurl them into the Atlantic. Fifty-four go over that day. Water is running low, the crew claims. Three days later, forty-two more are thrown overboard. Then twenty-six more, some leaping rather than wait to be seized. By the time it ends, at least 132 enslaved Africans are dead. Some say 142. Here's the reversal: this never became a murder trial. It became an insurance claim. Liverpool ship owners sued their insurers for thirty pounds a head — payment for lost cargo. The case, Gregson versus Gilbert, reached Lord Mansfield's court in 1783. Defense lawyers argued the deaths were no different than jettisoning horses at sea. A jury sided with the owners. Insurers appealed, won a retrial — and there the record stops. No captain, no crew member, no ship owner was ever charged with murder. The victims' names were never recorded. Only the tonnage was. Historians still argue over the true number lost. No one was ever punished.
