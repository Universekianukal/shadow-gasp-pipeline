November 22, 1987. 9:14 PM. Chicago. WGN-TV sportscaster Chuck Swirsky is mid-broadcast when the picture dies. Twenty-five seconds of static, then a face — a grinning mask, rocking behind a spinning corrugated backdrop. No audio. Engineers scramble, kill the signal, air resumes. Case closed, they think. Two hours later. 11:15 PM. WTTW, the city's public television station, airing Doctor Who. The signal cuts again. Same mask. This time it talks — garbled, distorted, ninety seconds long. It rambles about Coca-Cola, mocks a rival anchor by name, and near the end, someone off-camera bares their backside and spanks it with a flyswatter. Pulling this off took professional broadcast equipment and a signal strong enough to override two stations' microwave links — from somewhere with a direct line of sight to both towers. That's not a prank run from a garage. The FBI opened a case. Interviewed engineers, hobbyists, hackers across the city. Nobody was ever charged. No group ever credibly claimed it. Thirty-seven years later, the mask still doesn't have a name behind it.
