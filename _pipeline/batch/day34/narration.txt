December 1st, 1925. A radio operator taps out four words: "we are in danger." Then silence. The SS Cotopaxi never reaches Havana. It vanished two days after leaving Charleston, South Carolina — thirty-two men, five thousand tons of coal, gone. No wreckage. No bodies. No lifeboats. For ninety-five years it becomes one of the Bermuda Triangle's most famous ghosts — even inspiring a scene in a 1977 sci-fi blockbuster where it reappears in the Gobi Desert. Then, in 2020, marine scientists announce a discovery: a shipwreck locals have dived for decades, nicknamed the 'Bear Wreck,' sitting in just thirty-five feet of water off St. Augustine, Florida. Boiler placement. Hull dimensions. Everything matches the Cotopaxi. Case closed — almost. Here's the problem: the wreck sits barely offshore, nearly intact, in water so shallow a distress call should have brought rescue within hours. So why did thirty-two men die within sight of the coast? No logbook ever surfaced. No bodies were recovered, then or now. Some historians still refuse to confirm the identification. The ship that vanished into legend may have been thirty-five feet down the whole time.
