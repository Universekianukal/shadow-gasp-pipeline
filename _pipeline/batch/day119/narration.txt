May 17, 1904. A sealed railway carriage, guarded by twenty soldiers, rumbles through Moscow. Inside: 500,000 rubles in silver coins, destined for a remote leper colony. Only the stationmaster and the colony director knew the schedule. The train never arrives. No wreckage, no ambush, no tracks leading anywhere. Just a plume of steam over the forest, and then silence. The search begins immediately across 150 square miles. Five days of frantic combing yields nothing. Then, an abandoned clearing deep in the woods. A single railway spur ends in a pile of freshly-felled logs. The money car is gone, but the engine sits there, cold, its firebox dead. The coal and water are exhausted. An impossible phantom run. The soldiers swear they never stopped, never slept. But the physics are undeniable: the engine had been driven exactly far enough to reach this hidden spot. The investigation pins it on the stationmaster, a man named Perkhurov. He confesses, is convicted, and sentenced to hard labor. The money is never recovered. But in 1910, Perkhurov escapes from prison. He vanishes. And the half-million rubles—enough to build a hospital—remains hidden, lost to the woods and the plague it was meant to cure.
