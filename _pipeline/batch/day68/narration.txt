A prop in a Long Beach funhouse falls off a wall. December 1976. The arm snaps off and a human bone sticks out. A film crew from The Six Million Dollar Man is shooting inside the Nu-Pike Amusement Park. Someone touches the dangling arm. It's real. Police arrive expecting a recent murder. The coroner pulls the body down and finds something that stops him cold. An autopsy reveals arsenic embalming fluid, not used since the 1920s. Cotton stuffed in the body cavity. A 1911 penny and a 1924 ticket stub inside the mouth. This person has been dead for over sixty years. The body is so well preserved that officers initially assumed it was a mannequin covered in wax. Investigators trace the corpse backward through a maze of carnivals, wax museums, and roadside attractions. The trail leads to Elmer McCurdy. A train-robbing outlaw shot dead by a posse in Oklahoma on October 7, 1911. A funeral home embalmed him and put him on display, charging a nickel to see the bandit. No one claimed the body. He was sold. Sold again. Passed between shows for decades. Eventually nobody remembered he was real. He hung in a California funhouse, painted red, with a noose around his neck, while children poked him for laughs. McCurdy was finally buried in Guthrie, Oklahoma in April 1977. But one question was never answered.
