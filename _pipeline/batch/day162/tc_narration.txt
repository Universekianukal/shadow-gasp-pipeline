November 3, 1960. The radio operator aboard the cargo steamer Arctic Rose sends a final, routine position report. Then, silence. The ship disappears. A massive five-day search finds only one clue: an empty, drifting lifeboat. No distress signal. No wreckage. And most baffling of all—when the crew's personal effects are returned, every single officer's navigation log from the voyage is gone. Not misplaced. Not water-damaged. Systematically removed. The Arctic Rose was equipped with a state-of-the-art automatic log printer, spooling a continuous, tamper-proof paper record of every course correction and helm order. That spool is missing, too. The official inquiry calls it 'perils of the sea.' Case closed. But the sole physical evidence—that empty boat—was found 180 miles off the Arctic Rose's last known position. Too far for one lifeboat to drift in five days. The logs weren't lost. They were taken. Whoever took them knew exactly how to vanish a 455-foot ship and its 28-man crew. And they left the lifeboat as a message.
