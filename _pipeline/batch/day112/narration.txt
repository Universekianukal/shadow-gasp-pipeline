January 19, 1931. A phone rings at the Liverpool Central Chess Club. A caller named R.M. Qualtrough leaves a message for William Wallace — come tomorrow evening to 25 Menlove Gardens East to discuss insurance. Wallace is a Prudential agent. The lead sounds promising. The next night, January 20, Wallace takes two trams across Liverpool in freezing rain. He searches for over 45 minutes. He asks tram conductors, he asks bystanders. Nobody can help. Menlove Gardens East doesn't exist. There's a North and a West. No East. Never was. Wallace returns home at 8:45 PM. The back door is unlocked. His wife Julia lies in the parlor, bludgeoned to death. The weapon is gone. No forced entry. Only a small handful of cash is missing from a box that held nine pounds. Wallace is arrested, tried, convicted, sentenced to hang. Then the Court of Appeal overturns the verdict — the evidence is circumstantial, the timing too tight, the journey too public. He goes home to an empty house. He dies of illness two years later, never clearing his name. Someone called that chess club. Someone sent Wallace on a ghost errand. Someone knew his schedule, his routes, his wife. The call was traced to a phone box 400 yards from the club. Nobody ever claimed the name Qualtrough. Nobody was ever charged.
