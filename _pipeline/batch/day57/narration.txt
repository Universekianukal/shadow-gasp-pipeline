March 18, 1984. Two masked men break into the Osaka mansion of Katsuhisa Ezaki, president of Glico, Japan's largest confectioner. They bind his wife, torch the kitchen, and drag Ezaki into a waiting car. For twelve days, nothing. Then Ezaki escapes a warehouse in Ibaraki, barefoot, three days after his captors vanished. The case should close there. It doesn't. Weeks later, Glico offices receive a typed letter. The sender calls himself Kaijin Nijūichi-kansen — the Mystery Man of the Twenty-One Faces. He demands sixty million yen and threatens to lace Glico products with cyanide. Then packages of Glico candies start appearing on store shelves across Osaka and Kyoto, each laced with sodium cyanide, each bearing a warning label. Sales collapse overnight. Glico loses four billion yen. The Fox, as police nickname him, moves on to Morinaga, then House Foods. Every letter is typed on the same Toshiba typewriter. Every drop point is staked out. They never get a face. In one sting at Takatsuki Station, an officer spots a suspect in a white hat, gives chase — and loses him in the crowd. By 1985, the letters simply stop. No explanation. No arrest. In 2000, the statute of limitations expires. The Fox walks free, unnamed, uncharged, forever. The typewriter was never recovered. The twenty-one faces were never one.
