March 9, 1929. New York City. A laundry owner named Isidor Fink is found dead inside his own shop, locked from the inside. Two bullet wounds in his chest, one in his left wrist. No gun anywhere. The front door is bolted from within. The only other opening is a small transom window, barely 15 inches wide, blocked by dust and cobwebs nobody could have brushed through without leaving a trace. Police break in through a skylight. They find Fink face-down near the pressing machine, still warm. His wallet has $65 untouched. No robbery. No forced entry. No weapon. Coroner's report lists four gunpowder burns around the chest wounds, meaning the shots were fired at point-blank range, roughly two inches from his skin. But Fink's hands show zero powder residue. He never fired a weapon. Theories spiral for months. A hidden assassin who vanished through the transom? The opening is too small for any adult, and a child couldn't have reached the trigger angle. Suicide with a contraption to hide the gun? Investigators find no string, no mechanism, no rigged cloth. Detective Faurot calls it the most baffling case he's ever worked. Ninety-five years later, the room is still sealed shut.
