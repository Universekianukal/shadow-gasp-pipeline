March 1968. Soviet ballistic missile submarine K-129 vanishes 1,560 miles northwest of Hawaii. 98 crew. Zero distress signal. The Soviets search for two months. Nothing. But the US Navy's SOSUS hydrophone network had caught it — six implosions, then silence, at 16,500 feet. The Soviets never had that data. The CIA saw an opportunity. Not rescue. Recovery. A Soviet sub with nuclear missiles, codebooks, and torpedoes sitting on the ocean floor. They spent $800 million building the Hughes Glomar Explorer — a 618-foot ship with a giant mechanical claw designed to grab the entire 330-foot submarine and lift it through a moon pool in the hull. The cover story: billionaire Howard Hughes was mining manganese nodules from the deep sea. It worked. The press bought it for six years. August 1974. The claw descends 16,500 feet. It grabs K-129. It begins lifting. Three quarters of the way up — the claw fractures. Most of the submarine snaps and plummets back to the seabed. What the claw held — what they actually pulled aboard — remains classified. The CIA has never confirmed whether they recovered nuclear weapons, codebooks, or six bodies reportedly buried at sea with honors. The rest of K-129 is still down there. The Glomar Explorer was sold for scrap in 2015.
