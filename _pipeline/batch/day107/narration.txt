The most famous musician in America vanished into thin air on December 15, 1944. Glenn Miller — 40 years old, 17 number-one records, the man whose sound defined a generation — boarded a single-engine UC-64 Norseman at Twinwood Farm airfield in Bedfordshire. Destination: Paris. Five hundred miles across the English Channel. He was scheduled to perform for troops that Christmas. He never arrived. The pilot, Flight Officer John Morgan, filed no flight plan. The weather was deteriorating. Freezing fog. The Norseman had no de-icing equipment. No distress signal. No mayday. No wreckage. No bodies. Nothing. The Army declared Miller dead within weeks — not because they found anything, but because the paperwork demanded closure. Then the contradictions surfaced. An RAF navigator's log from that night records 138 Lancaster bombers returning from an aborted raid, dumping their payloads over the channel — directly in the Norseman's projected path. One crewman reported a small aircraft destroyed on impact. But the coordinates don't align with Miller's flight plan. And a German journalist later claimed Miller died in a Paris brothel — his death covered up to protect his image. Neither theory confirmed. No fragment of that Norseman has ever been recovered. Eighty years later, the English Channel still keeps its secrets.
