March 1995. The Royal Australian Navy patrol boat HMAS Bunbury spots a drifting freighter near Christmas Island. Its name is the MV Jian Seng. There are no lights, no engine sound, no signs of life. The boarding party finds a ghost. Three-thousand tons of rice, fresh food, and a table set for a meal. The main engines are cold, rusted solid. But the lights and generators are still on, a phantom current keeping the ship half-alive. The vessel was clearly at sea when it was abandoned. But how? No lifeboats are missing. There's no distress call, no logbook, and no sign of fire, flooding, or violence. The mystery deepens with its identity. The Jian Seng isn't in any registration database. Its name was crudely painted over a previous one. Its last known port is a dead end. The Australian government spends $1.2 million towing the ghost ship to Weipa. For six years, investigators comb every inch, finding zero clues. No crew manifests. No personal effects beyond generic clothes. In 2001, they give up. The ship is scuttled, sent to the bottom as a dive wreck. A perfect, 300-foot-long crime scene—if a crime even occurred—with no crime, no victims, and no answers. Just a massive, silent vessel that appeared from nowhere, carrying only cargo and an empty table.
