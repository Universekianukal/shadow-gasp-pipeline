A Dutch art dealer sits in a jail cell in May 1945, facing a firing squad. His crime: selling a Vermeer masterpiece to Hermann Göring for 1.65 million guilders. The painting, Christ with the Adulteress, was a national treasure. Selling it to Nazis meant treason. The penalty was death. Han van Meegeren had one defense. The Vermeer was fake. He painted it himself. Investigators laughed. Every expert in Europe had authenticated it. The canvas, the craquelure, the pigments — all supposedly 17th century. Van Meegeren insisted: he had baked the paintings, rolled them over barbed wire to crack the varnish, and mixed Bakelite resin into his oil paints to harden them into convincing centuries-old layers. Prosecutors demanded proof. So in January 1946, they gave him canvas and brushes in a guarded room. He painted Young Christ in the Temple — a sixth fake Vermeer, from scratch, while journalists watched. The treason charge collapsed. He was convicted of forgery and sentenced to twelve months. But here's what still haunts art historians: van Meegeren confessed to thirteen forgeries. Six were recovered. The others — Vermeers, de Hoochs, ter Borchs — were never found. Some may still hang in museums, labeled as originals. Nobody knows how many.
